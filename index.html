(Computational) Philologist

About

I am an assistant professor in computational philology at the University of Geneva, where I lead the FreEM project (resources and applications for Early Modern French) and FoNDUE (information extraction from historical documents). I joined the university in 2020 as a research and teaching fellow (Maître-assistant) in the chair of Digital Humanities.

After studying at Paris-Sorbonne (Paris IV) and the University of St Andrews, I wrote my doctoral dissertation in Latin philology at the University of Amsterdam (UvA) on the history of the stage actor in the Middle Ages. For my postdoctoral work, I turned to computational philology as part of a Swiss National Science Foundation (SNSF) project on Sévigné’s manuscripts at the University of Neuchâtel, where I created the first digital humanities courses. I also directed the Katabase project (study of the manuscript market in the late nineteenth century) and Gallic(orpor)a (development of tools for the layout and structural analysis of historical documents from Gallica).

My main research areas are early modern French philology, history of philology, seventeenth-century French literature, natural language processing, and automatic text recognition.

My open-access publications are available on HAL.

Projects

FreEM

FreEM corpora is a project dedicated to Early Modern French (16th–18th centuries). It provides corpora and NLP models for several core tasks, including lemmatisation, part-of-speech tagging, linguistic normalisation, and named entity recognition.

The project brings together specialists in NLP and philology in order to deliver accurate linguistic modelling, large-scale annotated corpora, state-of-the-art models, and linguistic studies of Early Modern French.

Gallic(orpor)a

The Gallic(orpor)a project develops a pipeline for mass digitisation of historical documents. It focuses on French documents written between the 15th and the 18th centuries, may they be manuscripts, incunabula or prints. The project addresses three main tasks: document layout analysis, handwritten text recognition and linguistic annotation (lemmata, POS tags and morphology).

Katabase

This project is at the junction of romance philology and computer science: by combining the needs of the first with the possibilities offered by the second, we plan to explore our capacity to automatically extract, structure and annotate sale catalogues to create a database of sold manuscripts in Paris since the beginning of the 19th c. Data will also be automatically analysed to identify same manuscripts sold multiple times over time, and reconstruct the history of manuscripts.
